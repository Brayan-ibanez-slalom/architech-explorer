Case Brief

1. Business Context

A regional Colombian airline is rebuilding its direct sales channel. The current platform is 10 years old [SAB-F26], the vendor no longer supports it, and every change takes months [SAB-F26]. Sales keep leaking to online travel agencies (OTAs): a booking abandoned at the payment step is usually lost, sending the passenger to an aggregator instead [SAB-F21], and direct sales carry a much better margin than agency sales, which is the entire reason this project exists [SAB-F22]. The new platform is not the airline's operating system — flights, seats, fares and cabin maps live in systems that will not be replaced [SAB-F25].

Reach

  • Flies inside Colombia and to five countries in the region, with US routes approved for month 18 [SAB-F10]
  • Web and mobile app, same capabilities on both [SAB-F27]
  • Around 65% of traffic on mobile [SAB-F11]

Scale (what the client told us)

  • 2,500,000 [SAB-F01] flight searches a day — ~30/sec average [SAB-F02], 200/sec [SAB-F03] at peak hour
  • 8,000 [SAB-F04] bookings completed a day, ~300 searches [SAB-F05] per booking
  • 25,000 [SAB-F06] post-sale actions/day (seats, baggage, upgrades, consultations)
  • 10x [SAB-F07] search spike within minutes of a promotion or disruption
  • 3x [SAB-F08] total traffic in December/Holy Week, for 2–3 weeks [SAB-F08]

3. Functional & Quality Requirements

Functional (what it must do)

  • Search flights and create a booking, holding the seats until payment confirms or the hold expires
  • Let passengers pick seats from the cabin map and add baggage, during booking or at any point after it
  • Support upgrade to business: release the economy seat, take a business seat, change included baggage, invalidate the seat already chosen, and charge the difference
  • Retrieve a booking by code and last name, as the entry point to seats/baggage/upgrade actions after the sale
  • Sell above physical capacity inside a margin commercial sets per route and season, changed without a deployment [SAB-F17]
  • Accept payment via PSE, whose confirmation returns asynchronously [SAB-F18]

Quality Attributes (how well)

  • Throughput ceiling awareness: absorb 200/sec [SAB-F03] peak search and 10x [SAB-F07] promo/disruption spikes while never exceeding the PSS's 150 req/s [SAB-F12] limit
  • Latency envelope: design around the PSS's 800ms p95 [SAB-F13], sometimes above five seconds [SAB-F13] response time — no end-to-end platform SLA/percentile beyond this was given (see Open Questions)
  • Seasonal scalability: sustain 3x [SAB-F08] total traffic for 2–3 weeks [SAB-F08] during the December/Holy Week [SAB-F08] peak
  • Continuity: no maintenance window on the sales channel [SAB-F09] — a scheduling constraint, not an uptime percentage; no reliability percentile was given, see Open Questions
  • Hold/consistency bound: seat holds are governed by the PSS's own 15-minute [SAB-F14] expiry, which the platform does not control
  • Modifiability: overbooking margin changed without a deployment [SAB-F17] — no propagation-time bound given (see Open Questions)
  • Delivery constraint: first release within 4 months [SAB-F16], built and run by 12 engineers [SAB-F15]
  • Channel mix: ~65% [SAB-F11] of traffic on mobile, same capabilities web and mobile [SAB-F27]

Why Each ASR Is Architecturally Significant (explicit justification)

ASRJustification
ASR 1 — PSS Capacity & Latency CeilingThe PSS is the only source of truth for a seat [SAB-F24] and rejects calls above 150 req/s [SAB-F12], with p95 [SAB-F13] latency of 800ms [SAB-F13] sometimes exceeding five seconds [SAB-F13] — and it "will not be replaced" [SAB-F25]. Every functional path (search, book, seats/baggage, upgrade) ultimately depends on this one external ceiling, so it cannot be solved feature-by-feature: it forces a structural read/write separation across the whole platform (per ATAM: a quality-attribute constraint that forces a competing design response), which is the definition of an ASR.
ASR 2 — Continuous Availability Under Variable LoadSelling never stops and there is no maintenance window [SAB-F09], yet load is explicitly non-uniform: 10x [SAB-F07] within minutes of a promotion or disruption, and 3x [SAB-F08] for 2–3 weeks [SAB-F08] every December/Holy Week peak [SAB-F08]. Meeting both constraints together — never pausing, while absorbing order-of-magnitude swings — rules out any design that assumes a quiet window to deploy, migrate, or recover in, which reshapes the deployment and scaling architecture itself, not one feature of it.
ASR 3 — Async Payment vs. PSS-Controlled Hold ExpiryPSE "confirms asynchronously, and you do not control when" [SAB-F18], while the PSS's seat hold expires at 15 minutes [SAB-F14] on a timer the airline does not control either [SAB-F14]. Two systems the airline does not own can each independently invalidate the other's assumption about booking state at any moment — this is not a single feature's edge case, it dictates whether the booking flow can be modeled as one synchronous transaction at all, which is a structural, platform-wide decision.

Decision 1 — Read-Model / Cache Layer in Front of the PSS for Search & Availability

Chosen approach. Serve the 2,500,000 [SAB-F01] daily searches (average 30/sec [SAB-F02], peak 200/sec [SAB-F03], up to 10x [SAB-F07] during a promotion or disruption) from a continuously refreshed read-optimized availability view, rather than forwarding every search directly to the PSS.

Rejected alternative. A thin pass-through proxy that forwards every search request directly to the PSS in real time, with no intermediate read layer at all.

Why the rejected alternative is genuinely viable. At low request volume this is the simplest possible design: one code path, always perfectly consistent with the PSS's own answer, no cache-invalidation logic to build or operate, and no extra infrastructure component for a 12-engineer [SAB-F15] team to run under a 4-month [SAB-F16] deadline. Many smaller booking systems run exactly this way for years without trouble.

What is sacrificed. Perfect real-time freshness — the read layer can be briefly stale relative to the PSS's true state — plus the ongoing cost of building and operating an additional caching/read-model component that the pass-through design would not need.

What is gained. Sustained call volume against the PSS stays within its 150 req/s [SAB-F12] ceiling and inside its 800ms p95 [SAB-F13], sometimes above five seconds [SAB-F13] latency envelope, even during 200/sec [SAB-F03] peak-hour search and 10x [SAB-F07] promotional spikes — directly protecting the conversion path that funds the whole project, since an abandoned booking is usually lost to an aggregator [SAB-F21].

Reversal condition. Revisit this if the PSS's contractual ceiling is demonstrably raised, or if measured p95 [SAB-F13] latency drops enough that direct pass-through can absorb 200/sec [SAB-F03] peak plus a 10x [SAB-F07] spike without ever breaching 150 req/s [SAB-F12].

Unresolved fact that could change this choice. The current traffic pattern and architecture of the legacy platform being replaced is not known [SAB-U05] — without it, we cannot tell how close today's system already runs to the PSS ceiling, which affects how aggressively the read layer needs to be refreshed. This also interacts with Decision 2: the read-model's refresh/poll cadence against the PSS and the write path's holds/confirms share the same 150 req/s [SAB-F12] ceiling, so an aggressively tuned refresh rate during a 10x [SAB-F07] search spike could itself starve the headroom the write path needs; how that shared budget is apportioned between the two paths is not stated in the brief [SAB-U12] and is addressed further in Decision 2.

Technology options: Open-source / portable: Redis or Apache Ignite as the read-model store, refreshed via a lightweight sync/poll worker. Platform / lakehouse-adjacent: Azure Cache for Redis or Google Cloud Memorystore as a managed operational cache. Hyperscaler-native: Amazon ElastiCache. All are read caches, not systems of record — the PSS remains the only source of truth for a seat [SAB-F24].

Trade-off: a managed cache reduces 12-engineer [SAB-F15] operational burden but ties the read path to one provider's networking/region model; a self-hosted Redis/Ignite cluster is portable across clouds at the cost of the team operating it themselves.

Decision 2 — Circuit Breaker & Bounded Queue for PSS Write/Mutating Calls

Chosen approach. Place a circuit breaker and a bounded queue with backpressure in front of every PSS-mutating call (create hold, confirm, seat change, upgrade), separate from the read path in Decision 1, since holds and writes cannot be served from a cache and must always reach the PSS.

Rejected alternative. Ask the PSS vendor to raise the 150 req/s [SAB-F12] ceiling, or negotiate a higher-throughput tier, instead of adding client-side protection.

Why the rejected alternative is genuinely viable. If the contract allowed it, this would remove the constraint at its source rather than working around it — no breaker, no queue, no added latency from client-side protection logic, and it is the most direct possible fix to a stated external ceiling.

What is sacrificed. The PSS is explicitly a system that will not be replaced [SAB-F25] and is described only by what it gives and what it costs, not as something the airline can renegotiate; pursuing vendor renegotiation would consume time the 4-month [SAB-F16] delivery window does not have, and there is no evidence in the brief that this is even on the table.

What is gained. Mutating calls degrade gracefully — queued or shed — during 10x [SAB-F07] promotional spikes and 3x [SAB-F08] seasonal peaks without ever exceeding 150 req/s [SAB-F12], which is what lets the platform keep selling with no maintenance window [SAB-F09] instead of failing outright when the PSS starts rejecting calls.

Reversal condition. Revisit this if measured write volume, even at a 10x [SAB-F07] spike, never approaches the 150 req/s [SAB-F12] ceiling in practice — at that point the breaker/queue is unnecessary complexity for a risk that does not materialize.

Unresolved fact that could change this choice. The PSE failure/timeout rate and typical asynchronous confirmation delay are not known [SAB-U07] — this affects how deep the write queue can safely grow before queued holds risk missing the PSS's 15-minute [SAB-F14] expiry. Separately, the 150 req/s [SAB-F12] ceiling is shared with Decision 1's read-model refresh polling, and no split between the two paths is given [SAB-U12] — this is flagged as an unresolved fact requiring an explicit rate-limiting/quota policy between refresh polling and mutating calls, so a refresh cadence tuned for a 10x [SAB-F07] search spike cannot silently starve the write path's share of the same ceiling.

Technology options: Open-source / portable: Envoy or resilience4j-style circuit breakers with a Kafka- or RabbitMQ-backed queue. Platform-native: Azure Service Bus with Azure API Management policies, or Google Cloud Pub/Sub with a rate-limited Apigee proxy. Hyperscaler-native: Amazon SQS with API Gateway usage plans / AWS App Mesh circuit breaking.

Trade-off: a managed queue/gateway shortens the 4-month [SAB-F16] build for a 12-engineer [SAB-F15] team but ties throttling policy to one vendor's configuration model; a portable OSS mesh (Envoy) keeps the breaker logic reusable if the platform migrates clouds later.

Decision 3 — Explicit Booking-State Machine With Reconciliation for Asynchronous PSE Confirmation

Chosen approach. Model a booking as explicit states (searching → held → awaiting-payment → confirmed / expired / failed) with a reconciliation worker that matches late, asynchronous PSE confirmations [SAB-F18] against the PSS's current hold status, instead of assuming the hold is still valid when payment resolves.

Rejected alternative. Treat payment as a synchronous step inside the booking transaction — block the booking flow and wait for PSE to respond before marking the seat sold.

Why the rejected alternative is genuinely viable. It is a simpler mental model with one code path and no distributed reconciliation logic to build, test, or operate — and it works perfectly well for payment integrations that genuinely respond synchronously. It is the default most booking systems reach for first.

What is sacrificed. The ability to hold the user-facing flow open for however long PSE takes, because it explicitly confirms asynchronously and the airline does not control when [SAB-F18] — a synchronous design would either time out incorrectly while payment is still pending, or hold server resources open indefinitely with no bound.

What is gained. Correct handling of the case where a PSE confirmation arrives after the PSS's 15-minute [SAB-F14] hold has already expired: the reconciliation worker can explicitly re-hold, refund, or fail the booking instead of silently losing the sale or double-selling the seat — protecting the revenue objective, since a lost or mishandled booking sends the passenger to an aggregator [SAB-F21] just as an outright abandonment would.

Reversal condition. Revisit this if PSE begins offering a synchronous confirmation mode or a guaranteed response-time SLA that reliably completes within the PSS's 15-minute [SAB-F14] hold window, removing the need for a separate reconciliation path.

Unresolved fact that could change this choice. No target reconciliation latency beyond the PSS's own 15-minute [SAB-F14] expiry is given [SAB-U08] — without it, the polling/retry cadence of the reconciliation worker cannot be sized precisely, only bounded conservatively.

Technology options: Open-source / portable: Temporal or Apache Camel/Zeebe as a durable workflow/state-machine engine. Platform-native: Azure Durable Functions or Google Cloud Workflows. Hyperscaler-native: AWS Step Functions with an SQS-backed reconciliation consumer.

Trade-off: a durable workflow engine (Temporal) is portable and keeps booking-state logic out of any single cloud's proprietary orchestration format, at the cost of the 12-engineer [SAB-F15] team learning and operating a new runtime; a managed step-function service is faster to stand up within the 4-month [SAB-F16] window but couples booking-state logic to that vendor's execution model.

Decision 4 — Controlled-Overbooking Margin as Externalized, Versioned Configuration

Chosen approach. Implement the controlled-overbooking margin as data-driven configuration per route and season, editable by commercial through a config/rules service, rather than as a value encoded in application code.

Rejected alternative. Encode the margin logic and per-route/season values directly in application code, changed through the normal engineering release pipeline.

Why the rejected alternative is genuinely viable. It is simpler to build — no separate config store, no versioning, no access-control model for who may edit a commercially sensitive number — and it is exactly how many smaller systems implement business rules successfully when those rules change rarely.

What is sacrificed. An additional moving part: a config/rules store, a change-history/versioning mechanism, and an access-control layer restricting who may edit overbooking margins, all of which must themselves be reliable and auditable.

What is gained. The margin can be changed without a deployment [SAB-F17], letting commercial react per route and season without competing for the same 12-engineer, 2-squad [SAB-F15] team that is simultaneously building the rest of the platform inside a 4-month [SAB-F16] window.

Reversal condition. Revisit this if margin changes turn out to be rare in practice — a low observed change frequency may not justify a dedicated config service over a fast-tracked, well-tested deploy pipeline.

Unresolved fact that could change this choice. The expected frequency of margin changes, and how quickly a change must take effect once commercial edits it, is not given [SAB-U11] — without it, the cost/benefit of a dedicated config service versus an accelerated deploy path cannot be finalized.

Technology options: Open-source / portable: Unleash or OpenFeature as a self-hosted feature/config-flag service with audit history. Commercial: LaunchDarkly. Platform-native: Azure App Configuration. Hyperscaler-native: AWS AppConfig.

Trade-off: a self-hosted OSS flag service avoids per-seat vendor licensing and keeps configuration portable, at the cost of the team running one more piece of infrastructure; a managed commercial or cloud-native service ships faster inside the 4-month [SAB-F16] window but adds a recurring vendor dependency for a business-critical commercial lever.

Decision 5 — Zero-Maintenance-Window Deployment Strategy

Chosen approach. Adopt a rolling or blue-green deployment approach with backward/forward-compatible schema and API changes, so releases ship while the sales channel keeps accepting traffic, because there is no maintenance window on the sales channel [SAB-F09].

Rejected alternative. A traditional scheduled maintenance window for deployments — a brief, planned pause in sales during low-traffic hours.

Why the rejected alternative is genuinely viable. Maintenance windows are simpler to reason about: no need for two application versions to interoperate during rollout, no backward-compatible schema discipline, and a scheduled short pause is still standard practice at many airlines. Nothing about the technology itself forbids it.

What is sacrificed. Simplicity of release engineering — every change must now be deployable without breaking in-flight bookings or holds, which means disciplined backward/forward-compatible migrations, feature flags for multi-version overlap, and more careful release sequencing than a stop-the-world deploy would require, adding process overhead for the same 12-engineer [SAB-F15] team.

What is gained. Selling never stops [SAB-F09] through the entire 4-month [SAB-F16] build-out and beyond, including through the 3x [SAB-F08] December/Holy Week peaks — preserving the direct-sales margin advantage that is the entire reason this project exists [SAB-F22].

Reversal condition. Revisit this if actual traffic data shows a verified low-traffic window where a short planned pause has negligible booking impact — but this would directly contradict the explicit "no pause" wording [SAB-F09] and would need explicit stakeholder sign-off before being adopted.

Unresolved fact that could change this choice. Whether a phased/canary rollout is acceptable given the no-maintenance-window constraint, or whether that constraint also rules out any blue/green cutover risk window, is not stated [SAB-U10].

Technology options: Open-source / portable: Kubernetes rolling updates with Argo Rollouts for canary/blue-green control. Platform-native: Google Cloud Deploy or Azure DevOps blue-green release pipelines. Hyperscaler-native: AWS CodeDeploy blue/green deployments.

Trade-off: Kubernetes + Argo Rollouts is portable across clouds and avoids locking the release process to one vendor's pipeline, at the cost of the 12-engineer [SAB-F15] team operating the cluster itself; a managed deploy service reduces that operational load within the 4-month [SAB-F16] window but ties the rollout mechanism to that provider.

Design Recommendations

9. Governance & Security Recommendations

The brief does not state that the platform handles regulated data explicitly, but a booking system inherently carries passenger name (used to retrieve a booking by code and last name), payment interactions through PSE, and — for the US routes approved for month 18 [SAB-F10] — likely travel-document data. This makes governance and security a candidate ASR-adjacent concern even though no compliance regime was named.

Is Governance/Security an ASR Here?

Partially — treated as a design concern, not a fourth ASR. The brief never states a regulatory driver, an access-control requirement, or an auditability requirement, unlike the PSS/availability/ async-payment constraints that are stated explicitly and repeatedly. Promoting governance to a fourth ASR would not be traceable to anything given, so it is addressed here as a required design section per this repository's rules, and the applicable compliance regime is listed under Open Questions rather than assumed.

Governance Recommendations

Ownership & stewardshipAssign a data owner for booking/passenger records, distinct from the PSS/fare-service/seat-map providers, since those systems of record will not be replaced [SAB-F25] and the platform's own domain (bookings, holds, post-sale actions) must have a clear owner among the 12 engineers [SAB-F15].
Catalog & discoverabilityRegister the booking, hold, and payment-reconciliation datasets with owner, freshness, and sensitivity so the small team can reason about what is authoritative here versus in the PSS.
Lineage & traceabilityTrace every booking-state transition (held → awaiting-payment → confirmed/expired) back to its triggering PSS or PSE event, so a reconciliation dispute (Decision 3) can be explained after the fact.
Data contracts & schema governanceDefine an explicit contract for what the fare service, seat map provider, and PSS return, since none of them are owned by this platform [SAB-F23], [SAB-F24], [SAB-F25] and any of them can change independently.
Data qualityDecide explicitly whether a reconciliation failure (Decision 3) blocks the booking (quarantine) or is queued for manual review — this is the same trust-vs-availability trade-off the course requires to be made explicit, and it directly affects whether "no maintenance window" [SAB-F09] holds under failure.
Quarantine pathRoute bookings whose PSE confirmation cannot be reconciled to a valid hold into an explicit review queue rather than silently failing or silently confirming — required by Decision 3's own reasoning.
Cost governance (FinOps)The fare service is paid per call [SAB-F23]; make per-call cost to the fare service and PSS observable, especially under 10x [SAB-F07] promo spikes, since spike-driven cost growth is otherwise invisible until the invoice arrives. No budget ceiling was given [SAB-U02] — flagged in Open Questions.

AreaRecommendation for this scenario
Data classificationClassify passenger name, booking code, and payment-reconciliation data as at least Confidential at ingestion, even though no explicit PII/compliance clause was stated in the brief — retrieval by code + last name is itself a low-friction lookup surface that deserves rate-limiting and classification.
Access control modelPrefer attribute/tag-based access for the small number of internal consumers (booking service, reconciliation worker, commercial config editors) over ad hoc per-table grants, so the overbooking-config editors from Decision 4 can be scoped precisely without a bespoke permission model per squad.
PII protectionMask passenger name/booking details in logs and non-production environments; tokenize or avoid storing raw PSE payment identifiers beyond what reconciliation (Decision 3) requires; encrypt in transit and at rest as a non-negotiable baseline for all traffic to/from PSS, PSE, fare service, and seat map provider.
AuditabilityLog every overbooking-margin change (Decision 4) with who changed it and when — this is a commercially sensitive lever that bypasses the normal deployment/review process by design, which is exactly the kind of change that most needs an audit trail.
Environment isolationNever use real PSE payment identifiers or real passenger records in development/test, especially under the 4-month [SAB-F16] delivery pressure where this is the shortcut most likely to be taken.
Secrets & keysStore PSS, PSE, and fare-service credentials in a secrets manager, never in pipeline configs, given that all three are paid/rate-limited external integrations [SAB-F12], [SAB-F23].

Anti-Patterns to Avoid in This Design

  • Silently dropping a PSE confirmation that arrives after the PSS hold expired, instead of routing it to explicit reconciliation (Decision 3)
  • Hard-coding the overbooking margin "temporarily" under the 4-month [SAB-F16] deadline, defeating the entire point of Decision 4
  • Treating a scheduled deploy pause as acceptable "just this once", eroding the no-maintenance-window constraint [SAB-F09]
  • Calling the PSS directly from every UI surface without going through the read-model / circuit-breaker layers (Decisions 1–2), reintroducing the 150 req/s [SAB-F12] risk piecemeal
  • Logging or storing raw payment identifiers beyond what reconciliation needs

10. Cost of Change — Where Do These Decisions Sit?

The harder a decision is to reverse, the more intentional we should be about making it.

Reversible Partially Reversible Near-Irreversible

Dependency on the PSS's own limits (150 req/s, 15-min hold) [SAB-F12], [SAB-F14]

Decision 3 (booking-state machine and reconciliation) sits closest to near-irreversible among things the platform actually controls — once financial reconciliation, refund logic, and downstream reporting depend on a given set of booking states and transition rules, changing that model requires re-validating every historical booking's meaning, not just a migration script.

The PSS's own limits are effectively fixed and outside this platform's control entirely. The 150 req/s [SAB-F12] ceiling, 800ms p95/>5s [SAB-F13] latency, and 15-minute [SAB-F14] hold expiry are plotted at the far right not because changing them is expensive to engineer, but because the airline has no lever over them at all — the PSS "will not be replaced" [SAB-F25]. Every decision above (1, 2, 3) exists specifically to build a resilient boundary around a constraint that cannot itself be moved.

Lock-in note (preserve optionality). The overbooking-config store (Decision 4) and the booking-state workflow engine (Decision 3) are the two components most worth keeping portable, since they encode business rules and financial-state semantics that must outlive any single infrastructure choice. Favor open/portable engines (Temporal, Unleash/OpenFeature) over proprietary managed equivalents where the 4-month [SAB-F16] timeline allows, and treat a managed choice as a knowing, revisitable trade-off otherwise.
