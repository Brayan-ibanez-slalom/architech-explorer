3. Functional & Quality Requirements

Functional (what the system must do)

  • Ingest engine temperature, GPS location, reefer-unit temperature, and engine fault codes from each truck's onboard device [NBL-F09]
  • Evaluate each reefer-unit temperature reading against the safe-range threshold supplied by the existing rules engine [NBL-F10][NBL-F12]
  • Route a breach alert to the responsible fleet manager
  • Continue delivering fault-code data to the existing on-premises system used by the warranty-claims team, unchanged, since it cannot be decommissioned [NBL-F08]
  • Persist historical telemetry so it can be retrieved for warranty disputes with truck/reefer-unit manufacturers [NBL-F04]
  • Present currently active alerts per truck/route on a dashboard [NBL-F11]
  • Present differentiated views of the data to fleet managers and to warranty-claims staff [NBL-F13]

Quality Attributes (how well)

  • Alerting latency: breach-to-fleet-manager delivery within 5 minutes [NBL-F03]. No attainment percentile given — see Open Questions.
  • Ingestion throughput: sustain telemetry from up to 3,200 trucks [NBL-F01], each reporting roughly every 30 seconds [NBL-F02]. Derived, not stated in the scenario as such: a full fleet generates approximately 9,216,000 [NBL-F01][NBL-F02] telemetry readings/day, calculated as fleet size multiplied by (seconds per day divided by the telemetry interval cited above). The 400-truck pilot [NBL-F05] therefore generates roughly one-eighth of that volume by the same calculation.
  • Durability / retention: historical telemetry retained for at least 2 years [NBL-F04]
  • Compatibility: the architecture (Decision 2, Section 7) is designed to require no modification to the existing on-premises fault-code system's inputs while it remains in service, since it cannot be decommissioned and has no committed migration date [NBL-F08]
  • Delivery timeline: pilot of 400 trucks [NBL-F05] operating within 4 months [NBL-F06]; full-fleet timeline and growth rate beyond 3,200 [NBL-F01] trucks are unresolved (see Open Questions [NBL-U02][NBL-U06])
  • Operability: buildable and operable by a 7-person team using Python/SQL, with only 2 members having production Kafka experience [NBL-F07]

Decision 1 — Event-Streaming Ingestion Path

Chosen approach. Adopt an event-streaming (publish/subscribe) ingestion layer that carries every telemetry reading as a discrete event, evaluated against the breach threshold continuously as it arrives, rather than collecting readings and processing them on a fixed schedule.

Rejected alternative. Scheduled micro-batch polling — devices (or an intermediate buffer) are pulled on a fixed schedule (assumption: illustratively, a schedule on the order of a couple of minutes), and breach detection runs once per batch.

Why the rejected alternative is genuinely viable. Micro-batch polling is simpler to build with the team's existing Python/SQL skills [NBL-F07], avoids operating new streaming infrastructure during a 4-month pilot [NBL-F06], and at pilot scale (400 trucks [NBL-F05], 30-second cadence [NBL-F02]) the data volume is modest enough that a tightly scheduled batch job could plausibly still land inside the 5-minute alert SLA [NBL-F03] with margin to spare. Many fleets run exactly this pattern successfully.

What is sacrificed. A new streaming platform to operate and secure, and a genuine skills gap: only 2 of the 7 team members have prior production Kafka experience [NBL-F07], so ramping the other 5 within the 4-month pilot window [NBL-F06] is real delivery risk, not a formality.

What is gained. A batch interval sets a latency floor roughly equal to the interval plus processing time, so as that interval is tightened toward the 5-minute SLA [NBL-F03] the safety margin shrinks; streaming removes that floor entirely and also produces the continuously-updating feed that Objective O2's "near-real-time visibility" and Objective O3's future predictive-maintenance ML foundation both need, without a later re-architecture.

Reversal condition. Revisit and collapse to scheduled micro-batch if pilot measurements show a 1-minute batch interval reliably holds the 5-minute SLA [NBL-F03] with comfortable margin, fleet managers confirm that a 1-minute-refresh dashboard satisfies Objective O2 once "near-real-time" is quantified [NBL-U07], and the team assesses that safely operating streaming infrastructure is not achievable with its current Kafka experience [NBL-F07] inside the remaining pilot window.

Unresolved fact that could change this choice. Objective O2 ("near-real-time visibility") was never given a number [NBL-U07] — if fleet managers would in practice accept a coarser refresh cadence (assumption), the case for streaming over batch weakens considerably, since the alerting SLA alone could plausibly be met by batch.

Technology options (capability first, then ecosystem): the capability required is an ingestion path that carries ~13.3 events/sec at pilot scale (400 trucks ÷ 30-second interval, derived — not stated in the scenario as such) and scales toward full-fleet volume without a redesign.
• Open-source / portable: Apache Kafka or Redpanda for ingestion, paired with Kafka Streams or Apache Flink for continuous threshold evaluation — directly matches the 2 team members' existing production Kafka experience [NBL-F07].
• Platform / managed OSS: Confluent Cloud (managed Kafka API) to reduce the operational burden the 7-person team [NBL-F07] would otherwise carry.
• Hyperscaler-managed: AWS IoT Core + Kinesis, Azure IoT Hub + Event Hubs + Stream Analytics, or Google Cloud Pub/Sub + Dataflow.
Selection driver: the team's existing Kafka production experience [NBL-F07] favors a Kafka-API-compatible option to reduce onboarding risk inside the 4-month pilot [NBL-F06]; the final platform still depends on the unresolved cloud-vendor decision [NBL-U05], so all three ecosystems are kept open rather than narrowed.

Trade-off: a new streaming platform and a real skills-ramp risk for 5 of 7 team members, in exchange for removing the latency floor that a batch interval would otherwise impose on the 5-minute alert SLA [NBL-F03] and the (unquantified) near-real-time dashboard goal.

Decision 2 — Additive Fan-Out Alongside the Legacy Fault-Code System

Chosen approach. Build the new platform as an additional, independent consumer of onboard telemetry — the legacy on-premises fault-code system keeps receiving exactly what it receives today, unchanged, while the new alerting/dashboard/warranty pipeline is fed from the same source in parallel.

Rejected alternative. A proxy/strangler topology — route all telemetry through the new platform first, then have the new platform forward a transformed feed onward into the legacy fault-code system, making the new platform the single ingestion point.

Why the rejected alternative is genuinely viable. A single ingestion point is architecturally cleaner in the long run: one lineage path, no risk of the two systems' views disagreeing, and it is the standard "strangler fig" pattern for retiring a legacy system over time. If the goal were ultimately to decommission the legacy system, this is the right direction to move in eventually.

What is sacrificed. Two independent ingestion paths into two systems creates a reconciliation risk — a fault code visible in the legacy system that is not reflected in the new dashboard, or vice versa — and it is not an end-state; someone will eventually still need to migrate the legacy system's ingestion, which this decision explicitly defers.

What is gained. Zero risk to the warranty-claims team's current dependency on the fault-code system, which is required because that system cannot be decommissioned and has no committed migration date [NBL-F08] — a proxy topology would require modifying or re-pointing the legacy system's input during this project, which the constraint rules out.

Reversal condition. Revisit and move to a single-ingestion (strangler) topology once a migration date is committed for the legacy fault-code system (none exists today [NBL-F08]) and the warranty-claims team has validated that the new platform's output already satisfies their existing consumption pattern.

Unresolved fact that could change this choice. No committed migration date exists for the legacy system [NBL-F08], and whether onboard devices can publish to two independent consumers directly (device-side fan-out) or whether fan-out must be implemented network-side within the new platform was never stated [NBL-U08] — this determines how much new integration work sits on the critical path.

Technology options: the capability required is reliable fan-out of the same event stream to two independently-failing downstream systems.
• Open-source / portable: a Kafka topic read by two independent consumer groups — one feeding a thin adapter into the legacy system's existing input, one feeding the new platform.
• Platform: Confluent Cloud with Kafka Connect sink connectors for the legacy adapter.
• Hyperscaler-managed: Azure Event Hubs consumer groups, AWS Kinesis with multiple consumer applications, or Google Cloud Pub/Sub with multiple subscriptions — the same fan-out semantics, vendor choice still pending [NBL-U05].
Selection driver: whichever ingestion technology is chosen in Decision 1 should natively support independent consumer groups, so the legacy adapter and the new platform can fail independently without one blocking the other.

Trade-off: an ongoing reconciliation risk between two parallel paths and a deferred (not avoided) legacy migration, in exchange for not touching a system the warranty-claims team depends on and that has no committed migration date [NBL-F08].

Decision 3 — Split Hot Operational Store / Cold Retention Store

Chosen approach. Separate a "hot" operational store — recent data, tuned for the active-alerts dashboard and the 5-minute breach-detection path — from a "cold" long-term analytical store — append-only, tuned for the 2-year-plus retention requirement and warranty/ML queries — rather than serving both needs from one general-purpose database.

Rejected alternative. A single time-series/operational database sized to hold the full 2-year history and also serve live dashboard/alert queries directly, with no second system.

Why the rejected alternative is genuinely viable. One system is simpler to operate, secure, and govern — a real advantage for a 7-person team [NBL-F07] — and at pilot scale (400 trucks [NBL-F05] over 4 months [NBL-F06]) the data volume is plausibly small enough that a single well-indexed database serves both needs without contention. Many teams run this successfully for years before it becomes a problem.

What is sacrificed. Two storage systems to operate, secure, and keep consistent, plus an archival/movement pipeline between them — real, ongoing engineering load for a small team [NBL-F07].

What is gained. The hot store can be tuned purely for low-latency recent-window queries that feed the 5-minute alert path [NBL-F03] and the active-alerts dashboard [NBL-F11], while the 2-year-plus retention data [NBL-F04] never competes with live alerting for the same compute or index budget — and an open, columnar cold-store format keeps the 2-year warranty archive itself portable regardless of which future platform is chosen [NBL-U05].

Reversal condition. Collapse to a single store if pilot-measured data volume and query patterns show one database comfortably serves both the live-alert path and 2-year retention without contention — a measurement, not an assumption — which would remove the archival-pipeline overhead entirely.

Unresolved fact that could change this choice. No fleet-wide growth-rate projection beyond the current 3,200 trucks was given [NBL-U02], so the point at which a single store would start contending for resources cannot be sized; this decision's split point (how large the "hot" window is) is PENDING VALIDATION against real pilot volumes, and no budget or unit-cost ceiling was given [NBL-U03] to weigh the archival pipeline's added cost against.

Technology options: the capability required is a low-latency hot path for "currently active alerts" plus a durable, open-format cold path for 2-year-plus history, with the whole 2-year retention window remaining queryable if regulation is later confirmed to require it.
• Open-source / portable: hot = TimescaleDB, Apache Cassandra, or Redis for active-alert state; cold = Apache Iceberg or Delta Lake tables on object storage (including self-hosted MinIO, for portability), queried via Spark, Trino, or DuckDB.
• Platform / lakehouse: Databricks (Delta Lake + Delta Live Tables for hot/cold tiering in one lakehouse, Unity Catalog for governance) or Snowflake (hybrid tables for the hot path, standard/Iceberg tables for cold retention).
• Hyperscaler-managed: AWS Timestream or DynamoDB (hot) + S3/Athena/Glue (cold); Azure Cosmos DB (hot) + ADLS Gen2/Synapse (cold); Google Cloud Bigtable (hot) + BigQuery/GCS (cold).
Selection driver: favor an open table format (Iceberg/Delta) for the 2-year retention store now, specifically because no cloud vendor is committed [NBL-U05] — the archive itself should not be locked to whichever compute engine is chosen later.

Trade-off: two systems to operate and an archival pipeline to build, in exchange for live alerting that never degrades as 2-year history accumulates, and a 2-year warranty archive kept in a portable format ahead of an unresolved platform decision [NBL-U05].

Decision 4 — ABAC / Tag-Based Access-Control Model

Chosen approach. Adopt attribute-based access control (ABAC), where access to telemetry, alerts, and historical records is granted by evaluating tags/attributes on the data (e.g., truck, region, data-sensitivity class) against attributes of the requester, rather than granting access through a fixed, hard-coded set of per-audience application roles.

Rejected alternative. Hard-coded, per-audience RBAC — build two fixed application roles today, "fleet manager" and "warranty-claims staff" [NBL-F13], each wired directly to a specific set of screens, fields, and queries in application code.

Why the rejected alternative is genuinely viable. With only two named audiences given today [NBL-F13] and a 7-person team [NBL-F07] building inside a 4-month pilot [NBL-F06], two hard-coded roles is dramatically less engineering effort than standing up a full attribute-based policy engine, and it would ship the pilot's access needs correctly on day one with no additional infrastructure to operate.

What is sacrificed. The extra engineering effort and operational overhead of running a policy engine (attribute definitions, policy authoring, decision-point integration) that a two-role RBAC model would not need at all during the pilot.

What is gained. The two named audiences, fleet managers and warranty-claims staff [NBL-F13], are correctly separated on day one, and the model absorbs whatever comes next without a redesign: the exact role boundaries for those two audiences were never finalized [NBL-U04], and the compliance regime governing this data is still under legal review with no determination made [NBL-U01] — either one could add a new audience beyond the two named in [NBL-F13], narrow an existing one, or impose a new access constraint (e.g., a data-residency or masking rule) after the pilot ships. An attribute-based model absorbs that change as a policy update; a hard-coded RBAC model would require re-touching application code and redeploying for every such change.

Reversal condition. Collapse to simple hard-coded RBAC if, once the compliance review concludes [NBL-U01] and the two roles are formally finalized [NBL-U04], no further audience or policy change is anticipated — at that point the policy-engine's flexibility would be paying for a change surface that no longer exists.

Unresolved fact that could change this choice. Neither the compliance regime [NBL-U01] nor the exact role/permission boundaries [NBL-U04] were given; if both were to land as simple, stable, and limited to exactly the two named audiences [NBL-F13], the case for a general attribute-based engine over two hard-coded roles would weaken considerably.

Technology options (capability first, then ecosystem): the capability required is centralized, policy-as-code attribute evaluation that can be updated without an application redeploy.
• Open-source / portable: Open Policy Agent (OPA) with Rego policies, evaluated as an external policy decision point callable from any service; or Casbin for embedded policy evaluation.
• Platform: Auth0 or Okta fine-grained authorization / attribute-based policies layered on top of their identity platforms.
• Hyperscaler-managed: AWS IAM/Verified Permissions (Cedar policy language), Azure ABAC conditions on Azure RBAC role assignments, or Google Cloud IAM Conditions — vendor choice still pending [NBL-U05].
Selection driver: favor a policy engine that is portable and callable independent of the eventual cloud-vendor decision [NBL-U05], since the compliance outcome [NBL-U01] and role definitions [NBL-U04] that will shape the actual policies are both still pending.

Trade-off: standing up and operating a policy engine that a two-role pilot does not strictly need yet, in exchange for absorbing an unresolved compliance determination [NBL-U01] and unfinalized role boundaries [NBL-U04] as policy updates instead of application redesigns.

Design Recommendations

9. Governance & Security Recommendations

The course lists security boundaries and data isolation among the capabilities that are expensive to introduce later. This scenario names two distinct audiences and an unresolved compliance question, so governance and security are treated as design concerns here, not something to bolt on after the pipeline works.

Is Governance/Security an ASR Here?

Yes — ASR 3 already captures it, and it is given a full architecture decision, not left as a recommendation alone. "Differentiated, governed access with no confirmed compliance regime" qualifies because the requirement shapes the access-control architecture itself: fleet managers and warranty-claims staff need different views of the data [NBL-F13], and vehicle-telemetry regulation is still under legal review with no determination made [NBL-U01]. If access is hard-coded per audience now, a later compliance finding forces a redesign rather than a policy change — that is a structural consequence, which is the test for architectural significance. No specific regime (GDPR/CCPA/HIPAA or otherwise) is assumed here, consistent with the source stating none has been determined [NBL-U01]. The resulting access-control architecture is Decision 4 (Section 7); the recommendations below are its supporting governance/security detail, not a substitute for the decision.

10. Cost of Change — Where Do These Decisions Sit?

The harder a decision is to reverse, the more intentional we should be about making it.

Reversible Partially Reversible Near-Irreversible

The cold-store table format (Decision 3) sits closest to near-irreversible: once two years of telemetry are accumulated in a given format for warranty evidence [NBL-F04], re-formatting that history is expensive and risks the very evidentiary trail the retention requirement exists to protect. This is why Decision 3 recommends an open table format now, before volume accumulates.

The access-control / classification model (Decision 4) is also near-irreversible once fleet managers and warranty-claims staff, and any policy the eventual compliance determination requires [NBL-U01], depend on it — retrofitting a different model means re-classifying every asset and re-validating every grant. Choosing an attribute-based (ABAC) model now, rather than hard-coded per-audience roles, is specifically what keeps this from being a full redesign the moment the compliance determination [NBL-U01] or the final role boundaries [NBL-U04] land.

The legacy fan-out contract (Decision 2) is partially reversible only while the legacy system remains un-migrated: today it can be changed because nothing but the legacy system's existing input depends on the new platform's shape. It becomes progressively harder to reverse as more new-platform consumers (dashboard, future ML [O3]) come to depend on the fan-out's specific event shape.

Lock-in note (preserve optionality). Because no cloud vendor has been chosen [NBL-U05], the storage format for the cold/retention store is the single highest-leverage place to preserve optionality — an open table format (Apache Iceberg or Delta Lake) keeps the 2-year warranty archive readable regardless of which compute platform the eventual vendor decision selects. The streaming engine and orchestration choices are comparatively replaceable.
